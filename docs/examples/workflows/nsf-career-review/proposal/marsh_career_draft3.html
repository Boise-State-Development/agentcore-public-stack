Project Summary

CAREER: Snow Drought and Groundwater Recharge in Semi-Arid Headwaters
Dr. Jordan Marsh, Owyhee State University

Overview

This project will deploy paired snow pillows and shallow groundwater wells across three semi-arid headwater catchments on the Juniper Peaks National Forest in southwestern Idaho: one snow pillow station and six monitoring wells in each catchment, logging snow water equivalent and water-table depth every 15 minutes for five winters. Drone LiDAR surveys will map snow depth across a one-square-kilometer block in each catchment four times each winter, and a distributed snowmelt-recharge model will be built, calibrated against the well records and run under snow-drought scenarios. The three catchments span an elevation gradient from winters dominated by rain to winters dominated by snow. The education plan brings rural high school students and their teachers, undergraduates and a doctoral student into the same measurements through an annual snow-to-groundwater field module run with a partner high school.

Intellectual Merit

Groundwater recharge from snowmelt is rarely measured at the same points where snow is measured, so its response to snow drought is inferred from streamflow or from models that have not been tested against groundwater. The project will provide paired, high-frequency records of snowmelt input and water-table response across a gradient of winter climate, and will test three hypotheses: that recharge efficiency (recharge per unit of snowmelt and rain input) falls in snow-drought winters because melt arrives more slowly and later in the evapotranspiration season; that the decline is largest in the catchment where winter precipitation shifts most often between rain and snow; and that persistent snow drifts mapped by LiDAR mark the locations of focused recharge. The calibrated model will translate these point and plot results into catchment-scale recharge estimates and show how recharge changes as snowpacks shrink and melt earlier.

Broader Impacts

Each June, 12 students and 2 teachers from Silver Basin High School, a rural partner school near the field sites, will spend three days in the catchments reading wells, probing snow-free drift scars and comparing their own measurements with the instrument records, reaching 60 students and 10 teacher participations over five years. Ten undergraduate researchers will be trained in field hydrology, a course module built on the project’s data will reach about 35 undergraduates each year, and one doctoral student will be trained in snow and groundwater hydrology. A pre/post survey administered by the university’s Office of Assessment and Evaluation will measure the module’s effect on content knowledge and science identity. All data and model code will be released publicly. The results matter most downstream: ranchers, irrigators and the town water systems below these headwaters depend on late-summer streamflow and shallow wells that are recharged by snowmelt, and annual briefings will give them the first direct measurements of how a snow-drought winter carries through to the water they draw in August.

Project Description

1. Introduction and Research Objectives

Headwater catchments in the semi-arid interior West store winter precipitation as snow and release it in a few weeks of spring melt. Part of that melt runs off, part is returned to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration, and part infiltrates below the root zone and recharges shallow hillslope and valley-bottom aquifers. That recharge is what sustains baseflow, spring discharge and shallow wells through the dry late summer (Halvorsen and Reyes-Tamura, 2019; Okonkwo-Bright et al., 2021). In the ranges of southwestern Idaho, where annual precipitation is 400–800 mm and summer rain is rare, the groundwater recharged in April and May is effectively the region’s summer water supply.

These snowpacks are changing. Snow drought — a winter whose peak snowpack is well below normal — can arise because little precipitation fell (a “dry” snow drought) or because precipitation fell as rain or melted early (a “warm” snow drought) (Abernathy-Cole and Pellerin, 2020). Warm snow droughts are becoming more frequent as the rain-snow line rises (Castellanos-Weir et al., 2023), and they are expected to be most consequential in the nival-pluvial transition zone. Streamflow records already show that catchments in the nival-pluvial transition zone respond to snow drought more strongly than higher catchments (Marrero-Quist and Haldane-Obi, 2023). How groundwater recharge responds is largely unknown, because snow and groundwater are almost never measured at the same points.

This CAREER project will close that gap with paired observations, scaled by drone LiDAR and generalized by a recharge model, in three catchments along an elevation gradient on the Juniper Peaks National Forest. It is organized around three research objectives and three hypotheses:

Objective 1 (O1). Measure snowmelt input and water-table response at the same locations, every 15 minutes for five winters, using one snow pillow station and six shallow wells in each of three catchments.
H1: Recharge efficiency, the fraction of snowmelt and rain input that reaches the water table, is lower in snow-drought winters than in normal winters, because melt arrives at lower rates and later relative to the onset of spring evapotranspiration.

Objective 2 (O2). Map snow depth with drone LiDAR four times each winter to connect the point measurements to the spatial pattern of snow across each catchment.
H2: Drifts that persist late into the melt season mark locations of focused recharge, so well-to-well differences in recharge are explained by local snow persistence more than by catchment-mean snowpack.

Objective 3 (O3). Build, calibrate and test a distributed snowmelt-recharge model and use it to estimate catchment-scale recharge under snow-drought scenarios.
H3: The sensitivity of recharge to warming is greatest in the lowest of the three catchments, where winter precipitation shifts most often between rain and snow.

2. Background and Significance

2.1 Snow drought in semi-arid headwaters

Snow drought has been defined in several ways, but the definitions share two pathways: a dry pathway, in which winter precipitation is below normal, and a warm pathway, in which precipitation is near normal but a larger share falls as rain or melts mid-winter (Abernathy-Cole and Pellerin, 2020). The two pathways have different consequences for the timing of water delivery. A dry snow drought delivers less water at roughly the usual time; a warm snow drought delivers water earlier, in smaller pulses spread over the winter, and leaves a thinner snowpack to melt in spring (Fairbanks-Oyelaran, 2022). Across the interior West the rain-snow line has risen by an estimated 150–250 m since the mid-twentieth century, and the area of terrain that alternates between rain and snow from one storm to the next has grown accordingly (Castellanos-Weir et al., 2023).

The semi-arid ranges of southwestern Idaho are a sensitive case. Their headwaters lie between 1,500 and 2,500 m, so a large share of the contributing area sits close to the current rain-snow line. Their snowpacks are shallow (peak snow water equivalent of 150–450 mm in a typical year) and wind-redistributed, so a modest loss of snowfall removes a large fraction of the seasonal store. And the downstream valleys depend on late-summer flow and shallow groundwater for stock water, irrigation and municipal supply, with little reservoir storage to buffer a poor year.

2.2 What is known about snowmelt and recharge

Mountain-block recharge from snowmelt is the dominant source of recharge for many basin aquifers in the semi-arid West (Halvorsen and Reyes-Tamura, 2019). At the hillslope scale, the efficiency with which melt becomes recharge depends on the rate at which it is delivered relative to the infiltration capacity and storage of the soil, and on whether the root zone is already wet when melt arrives (Ruiz-Halloran, 2018). Slow melt can favor evapotranspiration over drainage: when melt is delivered at low rates over a long period, more of it is held in the root zone and transpired, and less passes below it (Delacroix-Hune et al., 2018). Warming is expected to make melt both earlier and slower, because earlier melt occurs under lower solar radiation. Whether this slower melt reduces recharge in practice, and by how much, depends on soil depth, the timing of plant green-up and the presence of drifts that concentrate melt in space (Ostrowski-Bell, 2019).

Studies in the nival-pluvial transition zone have so far relied mostly on streamflow and on the snowpack itself. Streamflow integrates recharge, runoff and evapotranspiration over a whole catchment and cannot separate them; it also responds to recharge with a lag of weeks to months that varies from year to year (Okonkwo-Bright et al., 2021). Snow records describe the input but not its fate.

2.3 The knowledge gap

Three gaps limit what can be said about recharge under snow drought. First, few sites in the semi-arid West measure snowmelt and groundwater levels at the same locations and at the time resolution needed to see individual melt pulses reach the water table. Most groundwater observations in mountain catchments come from a handful of wells installed for other purposes, often far from any snow measurement, and read monthly at best (Hargreave-Tanaka, 2019). Second, point measurements of snow do not represent the spatial pattern of snow in wind-swept terrain: a snow pillow on a flat, sheltered site may hold twice the catchment-mean snow water equivalent, while drifts on lee slopes may hold four times as much (Ostrowski-Bell, 2019). Without a map of where snow accumulates and how long it persists, point recharge estimates cannot be scaled. Third, distributed recharge models are rarely tested against groundwater observations; they are calibrated to streamflow, which they can match for the wrong reasons (Pemberton-Asante and Rook, 2016).

These gaps matter most for the catchments that are changing fastest. Snow drought is already frequent in the nival-pluvial transition zone, but whether its recharge consequences are proportional to the loss of snow, larger than proportional (because slow melt is lost to evapotranspiration) or smaller (because rain and mid-winter melt recharge directly) is an open question that this project is designed to answer.

2.4 Preliminary observations

Since the winter of 2024–25 the PI and two undergraduate students have made monthly snow-course measurements (depth and density at 20 points) in each of the three study catchments during the access season, and have mapped spring seeps and snow-free dates from repeat photographs. These reconnaissance observations show three features that motivate the design. In both winters the lowest catchment had at least one mid-winter melt-out at the snow course, while the highest catchment kept a continuous snowpack from December to May. Lee-slope drifts in all three catchments persisted three to six weeks longer than the snow course. And seeps below the largest drifts in the middle catchment flowed into late July in both years, while seeps elsewhere at similar elevation dried by late June, which suggests that drifts focus recharge. The reconnaissance also confirmed winter access by snowmobile to all three catchments and identified candidate well locations on deep colluvium at each site. No groundwater measurements have yet been made; this project will make the first.

2.5 Why a CAREER project

The questions above need a sustained, multi-winter record, because the comparison at the center of H1 depends on sampling both normal and snow-drought winters, and because the education plan depends on students returning to the same instruments year after year. A five-year CAREER award is the right scale to build that record, to train a doctoral student through it and to establish the long-term research program described in Section 8.

3. Study Area

Here we define the term used throughout this proposal: by the nival-pluvial transition zone we mean the elevation band in which winter precipitation shifts between snow (nival) and rain (pluvial) from storm to storm and from year to year, so that the seasonal snowpack is intermittent rather than continuous. In the Juniper Peaks this band lies at roughly 1,500–1,900 m. Above it (above about 2,100 m) the snowpack is continuous from December to April in most years; below it, snow cover is ephemeral.

The three study catchments lie within 15 km of one another on the Owyhee Ranger District of the Juniper Peaks National Forest, about 90 road miles from the Owyhee State University campus in Rimrock. They were chosen to share geology (a thin colluvial mantle over fractured granodiorite), vegetation (sagebrush steppe with mountain mahogany and aspen stands, and Douglas-fir on north aspects in the higher catchments) and aspect, and to differ mainly in elevation and therefore in snow regime (Table 1). None lies within designated wilderness, and all are reached by Forest Service roads that are maintained in summer and travelled by snowmobile in winter.

Soils on the planned well sites are gravelly loams 0.6–2.5 m deep over weathered granodiorite. The reconnaissance in 2025 and 2026 located deep colluvial benches at each site where a shallow water table is expected within 2–6 m of the surface during and after melt; spring seeps below each bench indicate a perched or shallow saturated zone that the wells will intersect.

4. Research Plan

4.1 Objective 1: Paired snow and groundwater observations

Snow pillow stations. One snow pillow station will be installed in an open, level site near the center of each catchment’s well network in September–October 2028. Each station has a 3 m by 3 m fluid-filled pillow with a pressure transducer that records snow water equivalent, an ultrasonic snow-depth sensor, air temperature and relative humidity, a tipping-bucket precipitation gauge with an antifreeze charge, a four-component net radiometer and a soil moisture and temperature profile at 10, 30 and 60 cm. Data are logged every 15 minutes and sent hourly by cellular telemetry, so that a failed sensor is noticed within a day. The pillow gives a direct measurement of melt input (the decline in snow water equivalent plus rain), and the soil profile shows when that input wets the root zone.

Monitoring wells. Six shallow monitoring wells will be installed in each catchment, 18 in total. Two wells in each catchment sit within 50 m of the snow pillow, so that their water-table response can be compared directly with the pillow’s melt record. The other four lie along a hillslope transect from the ridge-side bench to the valley floor, with two of them placed below lee-slope drifts identified in the reconnaissance (Section 2.4) and two on wind-scoured slopes at similar elevation. Wells will be installed by a direct-push drilling contractor to 3–8 m, below the lowest water table expected in late summer, and completed with 5 cm PVC casing, a 1.5 m slotted screen across the expected range of water-table fluctuation, a sand pack and a bentonite seal, following the design of Hargreave-Tanaka (2019) for snowmelt-driven hillslope aquifers. Each well will hold a vented-cable or absolute pressure transducer logging water level and temperature every 15 minutes; a barometric logger at each pillow station corrects the absolute sensors.

Field schedule and quality control. Wells and pillows will be read manually once a month: by road from June to November and by snowmobile from December to May. Each visit records manual water levels with an electric tape, checks logger drift and downloads data. Snow courses at each pillow (20 depth and 5 density measurements) will continue monthly to calibrate the pillow against the surrounding snowpack. Soil cores collected at each well during installation will be analyzed for texture and water retention, and slug tests in each well will estimate hydraulic conductivity. Undergraduate researchers take part in every visit after training, and the high school field module (Section 5) repeats the manual readings each June.

Record. The network will produce five winters of paired records, from the first snowfall of winter 2028–29 through the melt of spring 2033, at 18 wells and 3 pillows, along with the supporting soil and hydraulic data.

4.2 Objective 1: Analysis

Recharge estimates. Recharge at each well will be estimated with the water-table fluctuation (WTF) method, in which recharge is the product of the specific yield and the rise of the water table attributable to an input event, after the recession that would have occurred without the event is removed (Szabo-Nakamura, 2017). Specific yield will be estimated for each well from the soil water-retention curves of its cores, checked against the drainable porosity implied by paired soil-moisture and water-level changes at the two wells beside each pillow (Brightwater et al., 2020). Because the WTF method is sensitive to the choice of recession curve, each well’s recession will be fitted from its own late-summer record, and recharge will be reported with an uncertainty range that propagates the specific yield and recession uncertainty.

Recharge efficiency. For each well and each water year, recharge efficiency is the seasonal recharge divided by the snowmelt and rain input measured at the pillow (or, for transect wells, the input estimated for the well location by the LiDAR-scaled snowpack of O2). Efficiency is computed for the whole season and for each melt pulse longer than three days, so that the effect of melt rate can be examined within years as well as between them.

Classifying winters. Each catchment-winter will be classified as a normal winter, a dry snow drought or a warm snow drought using peak snow water equivalent and the fraction of winter precipitation that fell as rain, both measured at the pillow, compared with the long-record climatology of the nearest automated snow station at similar elevation. Following Abernathy-Cole and Pellerin (2020), a snow drought is a winter whose peak snow water equivalent is below 75% of the climatological median; it is “warm” if winter precipitation is at least 90% of its median and “dry” otherwise. Because the three catchments sample different snow regimes in the same winter, each winter of the record contributes up to three catchment-winters to the comparison.

Testing H1. The primary test of H1 uses all 18 wells (six per catchment, n = 18) in a linear mixed-effects model of seasonal recharge efficiency, with winter class (normal, dry or warm snow drought) and seasonal mean melt rate as fixed effects and well nested within catchment as random effects. H1 is supported if efficiency is lower in snow-drought winters and if melt rate explains a significant share of that difference. The same model applied to individual melt pulses tests whether the rate effect holds within years, which separates it from other between-year differences such as antecedent soil moisture.

Testing H2. Using the transect wells, recharge per unit of catchment-mean input will be compared between wells below persistent drifts and wells on scoured slopes, and regressed on the local snow-persistence index from O2 (the number of days a LiDAR cell within 30 m of the well remains snow-covered after the catchment-mean melt-out). H2 is supported if snow persistence explains more of the well-to-well variation in recharge than elevation or catchment-mean snowpack does.

Lag and connectivity. Cross-correlation of the pillow melt record and each well hydrograph will give the lag from melt to water-table response and how it changes through the season, which will be compared with the seep and stream records to show how quickly recharge reaches the channel network.

4.3 Objective 2: Drone LiDAR snow-depth mapping

Point measurements cannot represent wind-redistributed snow. Objective 2 maps snow depth across a survey block of about 1 km² in each catchment, enclosing the pillow station and all six wells, using a multirotor uncrewed aircraft carrying a LiDAR payload. Drone LiDAR has been shown to measure snow depth to within 5–10 cm in open terrain and 10–20 cm under sparse canopy, at a fraction of the cost of crewed airborne surveys and with the flexibility to fly when conditions allow (Kettleborough and Amadi-Sorensen, 2021; Ferro-Lindahl et al., 2022).

Flight schedule. The first drone LiDAR flights will be flown in Year 2: a snow-off reference survey of all three blocks in September 2029, followed by four snow-on surveys of each block in winter 2029–30 (near the start of the accumulation season, mid-winter, near peak snow water equivalent and early in the melt). The four-flight schedule repeats in each winter of Years 2 through 5, giving 16 snow-on surveys of each block, 48 in all. Each flight covers one block in two to three batteries at 80 m above ground level and a point density above 150 points per square meter.

Processing. Snow depth is computed as the difference between the snow-on and snow-off ground surfaces on a 1 m grid, after co-registration on stable surfaces such as rock outcrops and road cuts marked with ground control targets surveyed by GNSS. Each survey will be validated against 100–200 probe depths collected along fixed transects on the same day; this validation is one of the main tasks of the undergraduate researchers. Snow depth is converted to snow water equivalent using the densities measured at the snow course and pillow, with a density model that varies with depth and time since snowfall.

Products. For each block and survey, the products are a snow-depth grid, a snow water equivalent grid, and an uncertainty grid. From the four surveys in each winter, the project derives a snow-persistence index for every cell, which is the input to H2, and a correction factor that relates the pillow’s point measurement to the block-mean snow water equivalent, which is needed to scale O1 to the catchment.

Data assimilation. The snow-depth grids are also the main constraint on the snow component of the recharge model in O3. Assimilating repeat LiDAR depth into a distributed snow model has been shown to reduce errors in modeled melt timing by a third or more in wind-affected terrain (Vandermeer-Achebe et al., 2024). The LiDAR schedule above is designed to bracket the accumulation and melt seasons in every winter in which the model is evaluated.

Flight operations. Flights will be flown by the PI, who holds an FAA Part 107 remote pilot certificate, with the doctoral student and an undergraduate as visual observers; the doctoral student will earn a Part 107 certificate in Year 1. Section 10 describes airspace, Forest Service notification and wildlife precautions.

4.4 Objective 3: A distributed snowmelt-recharge model

Objective 3 turns the point and block observations into catchment-scale recharge estimates and uses them to ask how recharge would change under snow-drought scenarios. The model has three components, all run on a 10 m grid over each catchment at an hourly time step.

Snow. A distributed energy-balance snow model in the form described by Pemberton-Asante and Rook (2016) is forced by the meteorological records from the three pillow stations, distributed by elevation and terrain. Wind redistribution is represented by a terrain-based drift factor, which is calibrated against the LiDAR snow-depth grids. Snow depth from each LiDAR survey is assimilated with an ensemble Kalman filter, following Vandermeer-Achebe et al. (2024), so that the modeled snowpack is reset toward the observed pattern four times each winter.

Root zone and drainage. Melt and rain reaching the soil surface enter a two-layer root-zone store whose capacity is set from the soil texture and depth measured at the wells and mapped from county soil surveys. Evapotranspiration draws from the root zone at a rate set by vegetation type and the timing of green-up, which is estimated from satellite greenness and from the soil-temperature records. Water draining below the root zone above a threshold storage, as described by Ruiz-Halloran (2018), is recharge.

Groundwater. Recharge enters a linear-reservoir representation of the shallow aquifer under each hillslope, whose discharge supplies seeps and the stream. Modeled water levels at each well location are computed from the reservoir storage and the specific yield estimated in O1.

Calibration and testing. The model is calibrated against the well hydrographs and the WTF recharge estimates from O1 for the first three water years (2029–2031) and tested, without recalibration, against the last two (2032–2033) and against the stream records at each outlet. Using groundwater rather than streamflow as the primary calibration target is the central methodological contribution of O3; it allows the model to be judged on the quantity it is meant to predict. Parameter uncertainty is represented by an ensemble of behavioral parameter sets selected by their fit to the well records.

Scenarios. Calibrated model ensembles will be run under scenarios that represent the two snow-drought pathways: a warm pathway in which observed temperatures are raised by 1, 2 and 3 °C and the phase of precipitation is recomputed, and a dry pathway in which winter precipitation is reduced by 10, 20 and 30%. The output is a set of response surfaces of catchment recharge and recharge efficiency against temperature and precipitation change for each catchment, which tests H3 directly: H3 is supported if the slope of recharge against warming is steepest in Cottonwood Creek.

Implementation. The model is written in Python by the doctoral student and the PI, building on code the PI developed for a single-hillslope application (Biographical Sketch, Product 1), and runs on the university’s research computing cluster. Code is released with each publication, as described in the Data Management and Sharing Plan.

4.7 Risks and alternatives

Dry wells. Some wells may not intersect a water table in every season. Well locations were chosen at seeps and benches where saturation is expected, and installation to below the expected late-summer level reduces the risk; a well that stays dry through a melt season still records that no recharge reached it, which is itself a result for H2. If a well fails, it will be replaced in the same summer from the replacement-sensor budget.

A run of similar winters. Five winters may not include every winter class at every catchment. The elevation design addresses this: the lowest catchment experiences snow-drought-like conditions in most winters, so the contrast between catchments is available even if the five winters are similar. The model scenarios (O3) also extend the analysis beyond the observed winters.

Instrument loss and access. Telemetry gives early warning of failures, and spare transducers are budgeted. Avalanche terrain is absent from the survey blocks, and winter visits follow the field safety plan in Section 9. If winter flights are grounded by weather, the four-survey schedule allows one survey to slip by two weeks without losing its place in the season.

5. Education Plan

The education plan is built into the research rather than added to it: the same instruments, the same field visits and the same data serve both. Its premise is that the water cycle in the students’ own valley — snow that falls on the ridges above town and reaches their wells months later — is the most direct way to teach how groundwater works, and that students who make real measurements that enter a real data set learn both the science and how science is done (Whitlock-Ibarra and Sandoval-Prieto, 2020). The plan has three goals.

E1. A snow-to-groundwater field module for rural high school students and teachers. Each June, 12 students and 2 science teachers from Silver Basin High School in the Owyhee Valley School District will spend three days in the study catchments. On day one they read the wells with electric tapes beside the logged records and see the melt that the pillow recorded months earlier arrive as a rise or recession in the well. On day two they survey the snow-free scars of the season’s drifts, which they will have seen on the LiDAR maps in the spring classroom unit, and measure soil moisture beneath and beside them. On day three they compare their measurements with the instrument records and present a short finding to the research team. Students camp two nights at a Forest Service group site with their teachers and the research team, and receive a stipend for their time. Silver Basin High School enrolls about 320 students, most from ranching and farming families in the valleys downstream of the study catchments, and has no field science program of its own.

E2. Undergraduate research. Two undergraduates each year will join the project as paid research assistants for 300 hours over the summer and academic year. They take part in the monthly field visits, run the probe-depth validation for every LiDAR survey (Section 4.3), help lead the June module and each complete an independent project (for example, the relation between drift persistence and soil moisture at one site) that they present at the Owyhee State University undergraduate research symposium. Recruiting will prioritize first-generation students and students from rural Idaho communities, through the Department of Geosciences’ introductory courses and the university’s first-generation student program.

E3. A data-driven course module. Beginning in spring 2030, the PI’s upper-division course GEOS 3410 Hydrology (about 35 students each spring) will include a four-week module built on the project’s own records, in which students compute recharge from a well hydrograph with the WTF method and compare it with the melt recorded at the nearest pillow. Because the records are released publicly (Data Management and Sharing Plan), the module will also be shared with the partner school’s teachers as a classroom unit. This course-based approach reaches many more students than individual research positions can (Nakashima-Ford, 2021).

Teacher partnership. The two participating teachers each year help adapt the classroom unit, so that by Year 4 Silver Basin High School has a self-contained groundwater unit using local data that it can teach after the award ends.

5.2 Evaluation plan

The education activities will be evaluated by the Owyhee State University Office of Assessment and Evaluation, which is independent of the PI and the Department of Geosciences and evaluates the university’s grant-funded education programs. The office will design the instruments with the PI in Year 1, administer them, analyze the results and write an annual evaluation report that the PI includes in the annual report to NSF.

Instrument. The main instrument is a pre/post survey given to each module cohort on the morning of day one and the afternoon of day three. It combines a 12-item content assessment on snow, infiltration and groundwater, written by the PI and the teachers and reviewed by the evaluation office, with the 15-item science-identity scale of Arroyo-Lindqvist et al. (2018), which was developed for and validated with secondary students. The undergraduate researchers complete the same identity scale at the start and end of each year, and the E3 course module uses a pre/post version of the content assessment.

Questions. The evaluation asks: (1) Do students’ scores on the content assessment rise from pre to post? (2) Does science identity change, and does the change persist? (3) Do teachers adopt the classroom unit, and how many of their students does it reach? (4) Do undergraduate researchers continue in the geosciences?

Participant tracking. Each participant is assigned a unique code at enrollment that links their pre, post and follow-up responses without storing names with the data. The evaluation office keeps the roster linking codes to contact details separately and securely. Module participants receive a short follow-up survey 12 months after the module, asking about later science coursework and post-secondary plans, and, with consent, the school’s counseling office reports whether each participant enrolled in post-secondary education. Undergraduate researchers are followed for two years after they leave the project through the department’s alumni records. Teachers report each year how many students the classroom unit reached.

Human subjects. The Owyhee State University Institutional Review Board reviewed the evaluation design and determined on May 20, 2027 that it is exempt under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(1), as research in an established educational setting involving normal educational practices (protocol 2027-0412). The determination will be renewed if the instruments change.

6. Integration of Research and Education

Research and education in this project are the same activity seen from two sides, and each informs the other. The research supplies the education plan with a reason to be in the field and with data that matter: the wells the high school students read are the wells whose records test H1, and the drift scars they map are the features whose role in recharge H2 tests. The education plan in turn supplies the research with measurements it needs. The undergraduates’ probe transects are the validation for every LiDAR survey; the June module’s manual well readings and soil-moisture surveys are a quality-control check on the loggers at the start of each summer; and the teachers’ and students’ questions about why one well rises and another does not have repeatedly shaped where the PI looks next. The course module (E3) uses the released data each year, which is a regular test of whether the data and documentation are usable by someone outside the project. Finally, the doctoral student learns to mentor by helping lead the undergraduates and the June module, which is part of the training described in Section 9.

7. Broader Impacts

The project’s broader impacts fall in four areas.

Rural STEM learning. Students in rural ranching and farming communities rarely have the chance to do field science, yet their families’ livelihoods depend on the water cycle that this project measures. The field module (E1) gives 60 students the experience of making measurements that enter a scientific record, in landscapes they know, with the evidence-based design described in Section 5. The teacher partnership leaves the partner school with a groundwater unit built on local data that continues after the award.

Training a diverse geoscience workforce. Ten undergraduate researchers, recruited with priority for first-generation and rural students, will be trained in field hydrology, instrument maintenance, drone operations as observers, data management and scientific communication. One doctoral student will be trained in snow hydrology, groundwater hydrology, remote sensing and modeling, and in mentoring. The course module (E3) brings real recharge data into the training of about 140 undergraduates.

Information for water users. The catchments drain to valleys where ranchers, irrigators and small town water systems depend on late-summer streamflow and shallow wells. Water users there plan each season from snowpack measurements, but no one has measured how a given snowpack translates into groundwater recharge. Each fall the PI will hold a briefing in the Owyhee Valley, with the local irrigation district and the town water operators invited, that presents the past water year’s snow and groundwater records in plain terms, following the practices of Lunde-Okafor and Prescott (2022) for communicating groundwater information to agricultural users. A one-page summary of each year’s results will be written for the same audience.

10. Permits, Compliance and Site Stewardship

Field permits. All three catchments lie on National Forest System land. The wells, snow pillow stations and survey markers require a USDA Forest Service special use authorization (research permit) from the Owyhee Ranger District, Juniper Peaks National Forest. Status: the PI held a pre-application meeting with the district’s staff in March 2027 and submitted the application on May 3, 2027; it is pending, and a decision is expected in spring 2028, before the requested start date. Each monitoring well also requires a state monitoring-well construction permit from the Idaho Department of Water Resources, which must be filed at least 30 days before drilling. Status: not yet filed; the permits will be filed in July 2028, after the Forest Service authorization is issued and before drilling in September 2028.

Drone flights. All drone flights will be flown under the FAA’s small unmanned aircraft rule, 14 CFR Part 107, by a certificated remote pilot (Section 4.3), within visual line of sight, below 400 feet above ground level and with a visual observer. The survey blocks lie in uncontrolled (Class G) airspace. The Forest Service application includes the planned UAS flights, as the district requires for flights launched from National Forest System land; none of the blocks lies in designated wilderness, where launching and landing aircraft is not allowed. The aircraft is registered with the FAA.

Environmental impact. The fieldwork has small, temporary effects. Each well disturbs less than one square meter at the surface; drilling uses a track-mounted direct-push rig on existing roads and two-track routes, so no new roads are built. Each pillow station occupies about 10 square meters. The Forest Service will review the project under NEPA as part of the special use decision, including a heritage review of each instrument location; the district has indicated that a categorical exclusion is the expected level of review. Well and station locations avoid riparian areas and known cultural sites; installation is scheduled for September and October, after the nesting season; and drone flights keep at least 1 km from the two known sage-grouse leks in the area during the spring lekking season.

Site restoration. At the end of the project (June 15 to July 31, 2033, in Year 5), every well will be decommissioned according to state well abandonment standards — casing removed or cut below grade and the borehole filled with bentonite grout — and the surface restored and reseeded with the district’s native seed mix. The snow pillow stations, survey markers and all other instruments will be removed, and each site will be inspected with district staff.

Equipment after the award. Title to the equipment purchased under the award (the drone LiDAR system and three snow pillow stations) will rest with Owyhee State University under NSF’s terms. After the award the LiDAR system remains in the Department of Geosciences for the PI’s research and for teaching in field courses, and the snow pillow stations, once removed from the sites, are kept for future research and for the field module.

Data Management and Sharing Plan

This plan describes the data and code the project will produce, how they will be documented and shared, and how they will be preserved. The PI is responsible for carrying out the plan and will report on it in each annual report.

1. Types of data and code

Snow and meteorological station data: 15-minute snow water equivalent, snow depth, precipitation, air temperature, humidity, net radiation, and soil moisture and temperature from three snow pillow stations (about 25 MB per station-year).
Groundwater data: 15-minute water level and water temperature from 18 monitoring wells, barometric records, monthly manual water levels, slug-test results and well construction logs.
Soil data: texture and water-retention curves from cores at each well.
Snow-course data: monthly depth and density measurements.
LiDAR data: classified point clouds for 48 snow-on and 3 snow-off surveys (about 400 GB in total) and the derived 1 m snow-depth, snow water equivalent, uncertainty and snow-persistence grids.
Model code and output: the snowmelt-recharge model source code, configuration files, calibrated parameter ensembles and scenario output grids.
Education evaluation data: pre/post and follow-up survey responses (see Section 6).

2. Data formats

Station, well, soil and snow-course time series will be shared as CSV files with a header block of variable names, units and time zone (all times in UTC). Gridded products (snow depth, snow water equivalent, uncertainty, persistence, model output) will be shared as NetCDF-4 files following the Climate and Forecast (CF) conventions, version 1.10. LiDAR point clouds will be shared in the compressed LAZ format with the ground control and processing parameters. These are open, non-proprietary formats readable by standard free software.

3. Metadata standard

Each data set will be described by metadata following the ISO 19115-1 geographic information metadata standard, with the CF conventions for variable-level metadata inside NetCDF files. Each release includes a readme that describes the instruments, their locations and elevations, processing and quality-control steps, quality flags, and known gaps, and the field standard operating procedures are released with the first data set.

4. Quality control

Logger records are checked against monthly manual measurements; drift-corrected records carry a quality flag for every value (good, estimated, suspect or missing), and the raw records are released alongside the corrected ones. LiDAR grids carry per-cell uncertainty from the probe-transect validation.

5. Sharing, access and timeline

All observational data and derived products will be deposited in a public repository, where they will be freely available without registration and assigned a persistent identifier that will be cited in publications and annual reports. Timeline: each water year’s station, well and snow-course records (October–September) will be released within 12 months of the end of that water year, and each winter’s LiDAR products within 12 months of the last survey of the winter. Model code, parameter sets and scenario output will be released no later than the publication of the paper that first uses them. All data will be released by the end of the award, August 31, 2033, regardless of publication status.

License. Data and derived products will be released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC BY 4.0), which permits reuse with attribution.

Code. The model code and the processing scripts for the station, well and LiDAR data will be released under the MIT license with each publication, with a version number matching the version used in the paper.

6. Human-subjects data

Education evaluation data are covered by the Institutional Review Board exemption described in the Project Description (Section 5.2). Individual survey responses will not be shared publicly; they are held by the Office of Assessment and Evaluation under participant codes, with the roster linking codes to names kept separately. Only aggregated, de-identified results (cohort-level means and distributions, with no cell of fewer than five participants) will be published and included in reports.

7. Storage and preservation

During the award, working data are kept on the department’s backed-up research file server with nightly backups, and raw logger downloads are retained unaltered. Deposited data will be preserved by the public repository for at least ten years after the end of the award. The PI will update the deposits if errors are found, with each version recorded.

8. Roles and responsibilities

The PI is responsible for the plan. The doctoral student prepares the station, well and LiDAR releases under the PI’s supervision, and the undergraduates help with quality control. The Office of Assessment and Evaluation manages the education data. If the PI leaves Owyhee State University, responsibility for the deposits transfers to the Department of Geosciences.
